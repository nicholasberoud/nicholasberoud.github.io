Borrowing at the Speed of the AI Build-Out

CoreWeave’s debt grew about {{hd_multiple}} times in under three years, faster than its assets. Its filings say far more clearly what lenders can claim than what the loans actually bought.

By Nicholas Beroud

The questions and interpretation are mine. Source archiving, data preparation and checks were developed with AI assistance under my direction. Every number on this page is generated from the study’s data files; hover over a figure to see the filing and page it comes from.

A GPU-cloud company sells computing time by the hour but pays for its chips up front, and those chips lose value fast. Something has to bridge that gap. For CoreWeave, the first company in this study, that something has mostly been debt. At the end of 2023 it owed {{hd_2023}}. By September 2026 it owed {{hd_2026}}.

The size of the debt is only half the story. A lender or bond investor wants to know what the money bought and what they could claim if things went wrong. CoreWeave’s filings answer the second question much better than the first. This case study follows the money across nine reporting dates, using nothing but what the company filed with the SEC, and marks exactly where the evidence runs out.

01Debt outran assets

Start with the narrowest sensible definition of debt: what CoreWeave borrowed, at the value on its balance sheet, plus finance leases, which are loans in all but name. We call this Headline Debt. It rose from {{hd_2023}} in December 2023 to {{hd_2026}} in September 2026.

A growing company is supposed to borrow more. The question is whether borrowing grew faster than the business it was paying for. Here it did. Total assets grew about {{assets_multiple}} times, from {{assets_2023}} to {{assets_2026}}, while Headline Debt grew about {{hd_multiple}} times. Debt went from {{hd_pct_2023}} of total assets to {{hd_pct_2026}}.

The definition matters. Count operating leases as debt too, and CoreWeave owed {{lid_2026}}, or {{lid_pct_2026}} of its assets. Since December 2024 the debt-to-assets ratio rose {{hd_pp_since24}} on the headline measure, but {{lid_pp_since24}} once leases are included. The next section explains why.

The third quarter of 2026 shows the mechanics. Headline Debt to total assets rose {{q3_change}} in three months. Splitting that change exactly: new borrowing alone would have added {{q3_debt_eff}}, and the larger asset base took back {{q3_asset_eff}}. With stockholders’ equity of {{equity_2026}}, Headline Debt was {{de_2026}} times equity. Net of unrestricted cash and marketable securities it was {{netdebt_2026}}, and that does not deduct {{restricted_2026}} of restricted cash, which is not freely available.

02The debt that doesn’t look like debt

CoreWeave leases much of the data-center space its GPUs sit in. Under US accounting rules a lease that has started shows up on the balance sheet as a liability: the present value of the rent still to be paid. Most of CoreWeave’s are operating leases, which credit analysts often leave out of debt. That choice alone is worth {{opl_2026}}.

Operating-lease liabilities grew from {{opl_2024}} in December 2024 to {{opl_2026}}. Finance leases, the kind already inside Headline Debt, never exceeded {{fin_2026}}. So the entire gap between the two debt measures is operating leases.

The balance sheet only counts leases that have started. CoreWeave also discloses leases it has signed but that have not yet begun: an estimated {{signed_2026}} of future payments in September 2026, up from {{signed_2024}} in December 2024. That figure is undiscounted, so it is measured on a different basis from the balance-sheet liability, and the two must never be added. One more lease, with payments still uncertain, is left out of the estimate altogether; its contractual maximum is {{excluded_ceiling_2026}}.

03From one kind of loan to a full capital stack

In December 2023 CoreWeave’s debt table had {{n_lanes_2023}} lines, and delayed-draw term loans made up {{ddtl_share_2023}} of the principal. A delayed-draw term loan is a committed loan the borrower draws in instalments as it buys equipment, rather than all at once. It suits a company that orders GPUs in batches against signed customer contracts.

By September 2026 the table had {{n_lanes_2026}} lines. Delayed-draw loans still grew, to {{ddtl_usd_2026}}, but their share fell to {{ddtl_share_2026}}. The rest came from the bond market and from suppliers: convertible notes ({{conv_share_2026}}), senior notes ({{senior_share_2026}}), and equipment and software financing ({{oem_share_2026}}).

Exhibit 5 shows every line of every debt table, so you can watch the stack being built. Each bar is the principal listed at that date (bar width grows with the amount). The dots on the delayed-draw loans show what each filing said about their security: by 2026 the older facilities’ pledged assets are no longer restated facility by facility. That is a change in what is disclosed, not evidence that the security was released.

The stack also changed shape in a way that matters to creditors. Most of the debt is recourse: if a borrower defaults, lenders can pursue CoreWeave itself. At September 2026 that was {{recourse_2026}}. A growing slice, {{nonrec_2026}} or {{nonrec_share_2026}} of borrowings, is non-recourse: lenders can claim only the borrowing subsidiary and the assets pledged to them. In December 2025 it was {{nonrec_2025}}. The largest non-recourse loan is DDTL 4.0, which section 6 looks at closely.

04What the money built

On the other side of the balance sheet, gross property and equipment rose from {{ppe_2023}} to {{ppe_2026}}. CoreWeave reports it in categories, the largest being “technology equipment”. The filings never say what is inside it, but for a GPU cloud the obvious candidate is GPU servers and networking, so we use it as a proxy for GPUs, not an equivalent. It was {{tech_share_2026}} of gross property and equipment in September 2026.

The build-out accelerated in 2026. From December 2025 to September 2026 gross property and equipment rose {{ppe_d_2526}}: {{tech_d_2526}} in technology equipment and {{dc_d_2526}} in the data-center group. These are changes in accounting categories, not purchases. In the third quarter technology equipment rose {{tech_d_q3}} while construction in progress fell {{cip_d_q3}}, which is consistent with equipment moving into service, but the filing does not give the transfer amounts.

Now put the two sides together. In September 2026 Headline Debt was {{hd_2026}} and technology equipment {{tech_2026}}: within {{tech_minus_hd}} of each other, and the two have moved almost in step since 2023.

It is tempting to read Exhibit 7 as “the debt bought the GPUs”. The chart cannot show that. Money is fungible: equipment was also paid for with operating cash flow and equity, some borrowed money bought capped calls, some notes were issued to pay for an acquisition, and operating-leased assets sit outside this line entirely. The match is a striking fact about the balance sheet, not a measurement of what the loans financed.

05Following the cash

Balances show the result; the cash-flow statement shows the flows. In the first nine months of 2026 CoreWeave raised {{debtiss_9m26}} from new debt (the issuer’s caption: proceeds net of issuance costs) and repaid {{debtrep_9m26}}, so net new borrowing was {{netborrow_9m26}}. That is {{netborrow_over_cfo}} times its {{cfo_9m26}} of operating cash flow, and {{defrev_9m26}} of that operating cash flow was the net increase in deferred revenue: cash received before the service is delivered. Cash spent on property and equipment was {{capex_9m26}}, {{capex_multiple}} times the {{capex_9m25}} of a year earlier.

Two things sit outside these bars. Equipment bought on credit from suppliers does not pass through cash at all: the statement separately reports {{ppeliab_9m26}} of liabilities related to property and equipment additions, which is shown beside cash capex, not added to it. And {{rouacq_9m26}} of new operating-lease assets arrived without any cash changing hands. Like the balances, these are consolidated flows: they do not say which purchase each borrowing paid for.

06What the loans bought, and what lenders can claim

These are two different questions, and the filings treat them very differently. Financed means what the borrowed money was spent on. Pledged means which assets the lenders can seize if the loan is not repaid. A loan can be secured by assets it did not buy, and a contract that permits spending on GPUs does not prove that it was spent on them. Below, CoreWeave’s financing is ranked by how strong the evidence of use is, from strongest to weakest.

  1. Strongest: financed and pledged both named

    Equipment-maker (OEM) financing

    The 10-K says CoreWeave entered into agreements with original equipment manufacturers “whereby the Company obtained financing for certain equipment”, and “granted a security interest for the financed equipment”. It is the one category where the filings name both what was financed and what secures it, and they are the same equipment. The balance was {{oem_2024}} in December 2024, {{oem_2025}} in December 2025 and {{oem_2026}} in September 2026, plus {{software_2026}} of software-licence financing.

    Limit: balances owed, not purchases; no GPU-only split; the September 2026 filing does not repeat the security sentence.

  2. Strong on contract, silent on amounts

    DDTL 4.0: the non-recourse project loan

    Signed on 30 March 2026 by a special-purpose subsidiary, CoreWeave Compute Acquisition Co. VIII (CCAC VIII). The credit agreement lets it “finance the acquisition and installation of Infrastructure”, a term that includes “any graphics processing unit servers”, and for each later draw other than top-up draws the borrower must give lenders purchase orders for the new equipment. Each quarter CoreWeave says the borrowings “are primarily used to finance the acquisition and installation of computing infrastructure”. Principal rose from {{ddtl40_2026q1}} in March to {{ddtl40_2026}} in September 2026, secured by substantially all of CCAC VIII’s assets: {{ccac_nc_2026}} non-current and {{ccac_c_2026}} current.

    Limit: the purchase orders are not filed, the customer and sites are redacted, and no GPU-only amount is given.

  3. Permitted use and stated purpose

    DDTL 2.0 and its 2.1 tranche

    Borrowed by CCAC IV under a May 2024 agreement that permits capital expenditure on infrastructure. CoreWeave says the delayed-draw loans finance “a portion of the purchase considerations, fees, and expenses relating to the acquisition of computing equipment”, a statement it makes for four facilities together. Listed principal in September 2026: {{ddtl20_2026}}, plus {{ddtl21_2026}} for the 2.1 tranche. Substantially all of CCAC IV’s assets and its equity are pledged, and CoreWeave guarantees the loan, so it is recourse.

    Limit: no amount is attributed to this facility alone, and no CCAC IV-only collateral value is disclosed.

  4. Cash, purpose general

    Senior notes

    For every senior-note issue outstanding in September 2026, the filings say the proceeds were “retained for general corporate purposes”. What the cash was later spent on is not disclosed.

  5. Part of the money bought a hedge

    Convertible notes

    Each of the three convertible issues paid for capped calls, options that limit dilution if the notes convert: {{capped_2031}}, {{capped_2032}} and {{capped_2033}}. That is borrowed money spent on protecting shareholders, not on equipment. The rest was for general corporate purposes.

    Convertible promissory notes

    {{promissory_face}} of notes (fair value {{promissory_fv}}) were issued to former shareholders of an acquired company as part of the purchase price, and later settled in shares.

The pledged side is better documented. The filings report the carrying value of assets securing the debt of “certain special-purpose subsidiaries”, the separate companies that hold financed equipment and customer contracts. That pool rose from {{spv_nc_2025}} of non-current and {{spv_c_2025}} of current assets in December 2025 to {{spv_nc_2026}} and {{spv_c_2026}} in September 2026.

07Where the evidence stops

These are the questions an investor would most like answered that the filings examined do not answer. They are listed because the gaps are findings too.

  • How much went on GPUs. No GPU-only dollar amount for any facility, for OEM financing or in total.
  • What each borrowing subsidiary owns. No breakdown of a borrower’s property and equipment by category.
  • DDTL 2.0’s collateral on its own. No CCAC IV-only collateral amount.
  • Who the DDTL 4.0 customer is, and where. Customer and data-center sites are redacted; the attached form of collateral agreement (Exhibit J) is omitted; the purchase orders delivered for draws are not filed.
  • Which subsidiaries are in the pledged pool. The aggregate is reported without naming its members.
  • Where the senior-note cash went. Retained for general corporate purposes; later spending is not itemized.
  • The bond documents. The senior and convertible notes were sold privately; their offering memoranda are not SEC filings and were not searched.

Coverage: EDGAR was checked on 20 November 2026. After the 10-Q filed on 12 November 2026, the only CoreWeave filing was a Form 4, which does not bear on these findings.

Definitions

Carrying value versus principal

Principal is the amount borrowed that is still owed. Carrying value is what the balance sheet shows: principal less unamortized discounts and issuance costs. Debt levels and ratios here use carrying value; the instrument mix uses principal, because that is the only basis on which every line is listed. At September 2026 principal was {{principal_2026}} and borrowings at carrying value {{borr_2026}}.

Finance leases versus operating leases

Under US GAAP (ASC 842) a lease that in substance transfers control of the asset is a finance lease: economically a loan to buy it, so it is counted in Headline Debt. Other leases are operating leases; their liability is still on the balance sheet, but many analysts leave it out of debt. Lease-Inclusive Debt counts both. Leases signed but not yet started are on neither.

Recourse versus non-recourse

With recourse debt, lenders can claim against CoreWeave’s general credit if the borrower defaults; CoreWeave guarantees it. With non-recourse debt, lenders can claim only the borrowing subsidiary and the collateral pledged to them, apart from limited guarantees for listed bad acts. The classification is CoreWeave’s own.

Financed versus pledged

Financed: what a borrowing actually paid for. Pledged: the assets lenders hold as security. Permitted use: what a contract allows the money to be spent on. The three can differ, and this study never infers one from another, or from debt and equipment growing at the same time.

The GPU proxy

CoreWeave’s “technology equipment” category, at gross cost. The filings do not define its contents, so it is a proxy for GPU servers and related hardware, not a measure of them.

Net debt

Headline Debt less cash and cash equivalents and short-term marketable securities. Restricted cash is shown, not deducted, because it is not freely available.

Undergraduate thesis · IE University · In progress

The Price of Fiscal Repair: Legislative Constraints and Sovereign Risk in Latin America

Working title
6 November 2026
Stage
Proposal under discussion with my advisor; public data access checked
Findings
None yet. No model has been estimated.
Expected
June 2027

When a Latin American government repairs its budget, do bond investors reward it as much as they punish a government whose budget deteriorates? And does it matter whether the legislature can hold the executive to its promises?

Proposed abstract

This proposed thesis examines whether improvements and deteriorations in primary fiscal balances are associated with symmetric changes in sovereign borrowing premia in Latin America, and whether these associations vary with legislative constraints on the executive. Stronger constraints may make fiscal commitments harder to reverse, but may also obstruct adjustment. The study will combine publicly accessible historical dollar-bond spreads, annual fiscal indicators and V-Dem measures, comparing fiscal changes across countries and over time while accounting for selected economic conditions and common shocks. A focused institutional case will examine how a fiscal commitment was approved, scrutinized and revised. The analysis will distinguish fiscal outcomes from deliberate reforms and interpret estimates as conditional associations rather than identified causal effects or direct measurements of repayment willingness. Building on existing research on fiscal adjustment and sovereign risk, it will assess a possible institutional extension to studies of asymmetric pricing. Final coverage and specification remain subject to data-comparability and literature checks.

A proposal for discussion, not a findings abstract.

Why it matters

A government can have the resources to repay and still not be believed. The thesis starts from that gap between ability and willingness to repay. Fiscal repair is costly, and investors have to judge whether it will last. Legislative oversight could make a commitment harder to reverse. It could also block the adjustment itself.

What the study will use

  • Sovereign risk: JPMorgan EMBIG dollar-bond spreads by country, as published daily by the Banco Central de Reserva del Perú. The outcome is the change in the spread, in basis points.
  • Fiscal position: the IMF World Economic Outlook (October 2026): primary balance, overall balance, gross debt and growth.
  • Institutions: V-Dem’s index of legislative constraints on the executive, v2xlg_legcon (codebook v16, pp. 53 to 54). It measures oversight in practice: questioning officials, investigations and opposition scrutiny.
  • Countries in scope: Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico, Peru and Venezuela, with Venezuela treated separately. The final sample and period are not set.
  • A case study: how one fiscal commitment was approved, scrutinized and revised.

Public access to all three sources has been checked. They have not yet been combined into an estimation panel, and documented statistical breaks (for example in Mexico, Ecuador, Argentina and Brazil) still have to be harmonized.

What it will not claim

  • Estimates will be conditional associations, not causal effects.
  • A narrower deficit is not the same as a deliberate reform. Growth, commodity revenue and accounting changes can improve a balance too.
  • A spread is a market price, not a direct measure of a government’s willingness to repay.
  • Asymmetric pricing of fiscal news is not new: Born, Müller and Pfeifer, and Pallan, study related questions. The possible contribution is whether legislative constraints condition it, and that is still being checked against the literature.

How it got here

The project began in October with Ballard-Rosa, Mosley and Wellhausen’s Contingent Advantage?, which asks when democratic institutions help governments borrow. The working title and the Latin American focus were set on 6 November. Earlier designs are retired.

Next steps

  1. Agree the design with my advisor.
  2. Harmonize the spread, fiscal and institutional data into one checked panel.
  3. Pin down exactly how the institutional extension differs from the closest studies.

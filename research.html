Contingent Advantage?First pass completed · Methods still to work through

Contingent Advantage? Sovereign Borrowing, Democratic Institutions and Global Capital Cycles, Cameron Ballard-Rosa, Layna Mosley and Rachel L. Wellhausen.

Author-hosted full text · First pass completed 27 October; methods skimmed

My question: What makes a government’s promise to repay believable, beyond having the resources to do so?

What stayed with me: The distinction between ability and willingness to repay connects the politics I studied with the financing decisions I want to understand. The authors examine issuance and amounts borrowed, and argue that the financing advantage associated with democratic institutions depends on global capital conditions.

Where it takes my thesis: I want to understand the mechanism before choosing an extension. A month without issuance could reflect a government’s financing needs or decision to wait, not just investors refusing to lend. I still need to work through the country-month models; I have not run the replication.

Note updated 1 November 2026 · Project: emerging-market sovereign borrowing.

Emerging-market resilienceRead and annotated

A Closer Look at Emerging Market Resilience During Recent Shocks, Hunter L. Clark, Jeffrey B. Dawson and Julian Gonzalez-Murphy.

Liberty Street Economics, 9 April 2026 · Read and annotated 23 October

My question: Why can the same global shock leave emerging-market governments with very different room to respond?

What stayed with me: “Emerging markets” is too broad a label to explain resilience on its own. The article’s country groupings made me look more closely at policy institutions, debt currency and financial buffers, rather than assume that every country responds like a commodity exporter.

What I still need to separate: Institutional credibility, debt structure and favorable external conditions can move together. The article’s comparisons motivated a question; they do not isolate the institutional effect I might want to study. This is a different outcome from the primary-market issuance in Contingent Advantage?

Note updated 1 November 2026 · Project: emerging-market sovereign borrowing.

The data behind Treasury ownershipSelected methodology sections read

Foreign Portfolio Holdings of U.S. Securities, U.S. Treasury / Federal Reserve.

Survey as of 30 June 2025 · Selected methodology sections read 1 November 2026

My question: What do ownership statistics actually tell me about who holds Treasury debt?

What stayed with me: Individual security records can reveal discrepancies hidden by an aggregate total. The distinction between official and private holders also made me reconsider how quickly I had connected an investor label with a motive.

Where the work stands: The public-table series has now been assembled and checked with AI-assisted code. I am working through the interpretation. Holdings do not directly measure price sensitivity, and a custodian’s location is not necessarily the ultimate investor’s residence.

The Deficit MythReading in progress

The Deficit Myth, Stephanie Kelton.

Reading in progress

My question: Which constraints matter when a government issues its own currency, and how should that change the way I read a fiscal-risk headline?

What stayed with me: The opening discussion of currency issuance and inflation pushed me to distinguish the size of a deficit from an explanation of its economic consequences. It also brought me back to the question of why foreign investors hold Treasuries.

Still open: I want to compare the argument with monetary institutions and market evidence. This is a reading reflection, not a completed application of modern monetary theory or an explanation established by my term-premium results.

Note updated 1 November 2026 · Project: Treasury fiscal-risk research.

Article ideas to developOutlines, separate from published research

Possible follow-ups, not adopted research designs or results:

  • What moved Treasury yields? A dated split of 10-year yield changes into expected rates and term premium, episode by episode.
  • From yields to the interest bill. How quickly higher rates pass through to federal interest costs as debt is refinanced.
  • Who holds the debt. The first descriptive series is now available in the TIC working study. Testing whether ownership explains fiscal sensitivity would require a separate design. The background is in Who Buys the Debt?
  • A fiscal-news diary. Market moves around one family of events, such as debt-limit deadlines or refunding announcements.
  • France as a separate case. The French-German ten-year spread measures something different from the US term premium, so it needs its own design rather than a copy of this one.

How much have the specialised GPU-cloud companies borrowed to build out AI computing capacity, how has their leverage changed, and what assets has that borrowing financed or secured?

Why it matters

Neoclouds such as CoreWeave rent GPU computing capacity to AI developers, and they have used debt to pay for their expansion. For a lender or bond investor the useful questions are concrete: how much has actually been borrowed, by which legal entity, against which assets and on what terms. A headline financing announcement rarely answers them. A facility can be larger than the amount drawn, can replace older debt and can sit in a subsidiary whose creditors have a claim on a specific pool of equipment and contracts.

The question and the scope

The assignment covers five companies: CoreWeave, Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe and Nscale. The work proceeds one company at a time, starting with CoreWeave, because as a listed company it files detailed disclosures and its financing agreements are on record. It has three connected parts:

  • Debt and leverage: borrowing over time and, where comparable statements exist, relative to assets and equity.
  • Assets and collateral: what the borrowing paid for (GPUs, data-centre property or other investment) and which assets secure it.
  • Financing terms: the borrower, maturities, cost, covenants and security, where they are disclosed.

Four measurement choices

Most of the difficulty is in deciding what counts. The project fixes these rules before any number is entered:

  1. Outstanding debt is not facility capacity. Debt drawn, undrawn commitments and announced financing are recorded as different things. A new facility may refinance old debt rather than add its full headline amount.
  2. Match the entity and the date. A subsidiary’s facility is not added to a consolidated total that already includes it, and one entity’s debt is not divided by another entity’s assets.
  3. Keep leases visible. Finance and operating leases stay separate from borrowing, and owned equipment is kept apart from leased right-of-use assets.
  4. Financed is not the same as pledged. What a loan paid for and what secures it can differ. A financing agreement’s permitted uses do not show how every dollar was spent, and where cash is fungible the allocation is recorded as unresolved rather than inferred.

A missing disclosure is treated as a gap in coverage, not as a zero.

How the evidence is built

For CoreWeave, a listed company, the evidence is its own filings: annual and quarterly reports, current reports and the financing agreements filed as exhibits. Each original document is archived unchanged with a checksum. Every figure entered must point to a specific document and page, and each record has to pass a separate check against that source, independent of the step that prepared it, before it can be used in an analysis. Several of the other companies are privately held, so which disclosures can support comparable figures for them is still an open question.

Where it stands

  • November 2026: scope agreed with Professor Gete; work begins with CoreWeave.
  • Done for CoreWeave: its filings and financing agreements are archived, the research design links debt to investment and collateral, and the agreements’ key terms (permitted use, security, guarantees and covenants) are indexed by clause and page.
  • In progress: a first set of debt-history records, waiting for the independent source check.
  • Not started: Nebius, Lambda, Crusoe and Nscale.

What this page does not show

  • No figures, ratios or comparisons yet. They will appear only after they are checked.
  • When they do, they will be descriptive. They will not establish credit quality, default probabilities or the future value of computing capacity.

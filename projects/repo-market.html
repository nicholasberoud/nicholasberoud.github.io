Repo Market Model

Reading pressure in overnight Treasury funding

Evidence prepared 1 October 2026 · Website adaptation reviewed 1 November 2026

A higher overnight borrowing rate is an observation. Understanding what it says about the supply of cash is a separate question.

Eleonora Björnberg’s project studies pressure in the market where cash is borrowed against securities, including US Treasuries. My contribution was economic interpretation and public-data selection: what should a pressure threshold mean, and which balance-sheet movements might help explain it?

This is a readable adaptation of my October advisor evidence pack. It presents historical observations and the questions I took from them. Eleonora leads the forecasting model’s research design, implementation and evaluation.

What does a pressure threshold mean?

SOFR measures the cost of overnight cash borrowing secured by Treasury securities. IORB is the rate the Fed pays on eligible reserve balances. Subtracting the second from the first puts the market borrowing cost in relation to an administered rate. One basis point is 0.01 percentage point.

The same spread can be unusual in one period and common in another. In this sample, the median was +1 bp in 2018–19 and −10 bp in 2021–23. That is useful context when considering fixed thresholds such as +5 or +10 bp; it does not establish which threshold makes the best forecast.

A limitation caught in review

The original memo’s percentages of days above the thresholds contain a numerical boundary error: some readings exactly at 5 or 10 bp were excluded. Those percentages are not reproduced here. The medians and percentiles above were checked against the frozen data and are unaffected.

What happened around the October 2025 spike?

The spread reached 32 bp on 31 October 2025. The evidence pack looked at Treasury cash, bank reserves and overnight reverse repo balances around that episode. Putting their observation dates beside the numbers matters: the first two series are weekly averages, while reverse repo usage is daily.

My interpretation was that Treasury cash management and the available liquidity buffer deserved attention together. But a reserve decline alongside a spread spike does not establish that one caused the other. Month-end financing conditions also matter, and one episode cannot validate a leading indicator.

Does the reverse-repo balance change the picture?

The overnight reverse repo facility gives eligible counterparties a way to invest cash at the Fed. The pack grouped weeks by that facility’s balance and compared the median funding spread in each group.

Lower facility balances coincided with higher median spreads. The important qualification is that the low-balance group pools very different periods, including 2018–19 and 2025–26. This comparison motivates a question about reserve conditions; it does not prove a $100 billion tipping point or show that facility balances independently predict tomorrow’s spread.

How do the Fed’s facilities fit in?

The memo also asks how the standing repo facility’s rate relates to IORB. Its full rate table uses the upper bound of the federal funds target range as a proxy, with selected dates checked against FOMC records. That proxy should not be read as an independently verified history of facility rates.

The practical lesson for my contribution is to understand each series before using it. An administered rate, a market rate, a balance-sheet stock and a weekly average answer different questions. The same distinction applies to the memo’s fiscal discussion: a possible transmission mechanism is not a forecast result.

Data snapshot: FRED downloads dated 1 October 2026. These are revised historical data, not the information available to a forecaster on each past date. Public series: SOFR, IOER, IORB, ON RRP, reserve balances and Treasury cash.

Review, 1 November 2026: the website review reproduced the displayed medians and percentiles from the saved observations. Inspectability: the downloads contain the displayed aggregate tables, not the raw data pipeline. The standalone generation code remains private. Table provenance and adaptation notes.

About The Long End

I came to this work through political economy. I wanted to understand why some governments face immediate pressure from creditors while others can keep borrowing on comparatively favorable terms. The question is about institutions and credibility, but also about which risks a bond price captures.

Headlines about rising U.S. debt led me to a narrower question I could examine: has the relationship between fiscal balances and the estimated Treasury term premium stayed stable? That became the first quantitative study here. It required choices about fiscal-year timing, repeated annual observations, controls and the model used to estimate the premium.

The result made the question more interesting. A weaker estimated relationship does not mean fiscal risk has disappeared. It could reflect changes in economic conditions, policy, investor demand or the model itself. The follow-up work starts with those distinctions, rather than assuming that one explanation has already won.

I began the underlying research in September 2026 and opened the journal in October. Alongside the Treasury work, I use it for market commentary and explainers. My undergraduate thesis on political credibility and sovereign borrowing has its own developing question and research design.

What the term premium measures

Buy a 10-year Treasury, or roll three-month bills for ten years? The 10-year yield is the expected path of short rates plus the term premium: the extra you’re paid for locking in, against the risk that rates, inflation or the government’s finances turn out differently.

10-year yield = expected path of short rates + term premium

Nobody observes the premium directly. Models estimate it, and different models disagree. Every analysis here says which one it uses, what it can show and what it can’t.

How each analysis works

For each piece I aim to make the question, evidence and limits easy to follow. The Treasury study includes downloadable results, chart data and a methods appendix; commentary is labeled separately. The research desk records selected readings and questions before they become finished articles. A new idea is not an empirical result.

Questions at the Research Desk

Work on yield decompositions, refinancing and fiscal news is still at the idea stage. I keep those open questions and outlines at the Research Desk, alongside the reading that may shape them.

Data and code

The analysis uses public data from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the Federal Reserve Board, FRED and the Treasury. The Treasury term-premium and Brazil code is kept in a private research repository; those articles link their generated results and chart data, with version information in the results files. Their downloads are not a public release of the full research pipeline. The separate TIC working note includes its own data, methods and a public code snapshot with reproduction instructions.

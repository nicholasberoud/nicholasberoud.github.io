About The Long End

“I used to think that if there was reincarnation, I wanted to come back as the president or the pope or a .400 baseball hitter. But now I want to come back as the bond market. You can intimidate everybody.”

James Carville, quoted in The Wall Street Journal, 25 February 1993

Where did the bond vigilantes go, and are they coming back?

This started with a question I couldn’t shake. Through September and October 2026 I kept returning to the same arithmetic: a debt stock far larger than in 2007, an interest bill climbing toward record shares of GDP, 10-year yields back near pre-crisis levels. Then the term premium broke out to its highest level in twelve years. If fiscal risk is real, why did the bond market spend fifteen years barely charging for it, and is it starting to charge again?

The arithmetic is simple enough to draw. A government’s interest bill is a rectangle: the stock of debt along one side, the average interest rate on that debt along the other. For most of the 2010s the rate side was thin enough that the debt side could keep growing without the area getting out of hand. Now both sides are long, and the rate side keeps catching up with market yields every time old debt is refinanced.

Whether that rectangle becomes a problem comes down to a race between two numbers: r, the average rate the government pays on its debt, and g, the growth rate of the economy. When g wins, the economy can outgrow its debt, as long as the budget before interest isn’t too far in deficit. When r wins, the debt ratio climbs on its own unless the government runs surpluses before interest.

The end of that road has a name: fiscal dominance. It’s the point where the debt is so large that the central bank can’t fight inflation without risking a debt crisis, because every rate hike also raises the government’s interest bill. You don’t have to believe the US is there to see why the question matters. If investors start to think it’s coming, one of the first places it should show up is the price of lending long.

That price has a name too

Buy a 10-year Treasury, or roll three-month bills for ten years? The 10-year yield is the expected path of short rates plus the term premium: the extra you’re paid for locking in, against the risk that rates, inflation or the government’s finances turn out differently.

10-year yield = expected path of short rates + term premium

Nobody observes the premium directly. Models estimate it, and different models disagree. Every analysis here says which one it uses, what it can show and what it can’t.

How each analysis works

Every piece follows the same path: the question and why it matters now, the dated evidence, my interpretation, the strongest competing explanation, what the evidence cannot establish, what would change my mind, and the sources and data behind every number. Stress tests are declared before they are run, and every number links to the file it came from.

Coming up

Open questions, not results. Each becomes its own piece once it has a design and data behind it.

  • What moved Treasury yields? A dated split of 10-year yield changes into expected rates and term premium, episode by episode.
  • From yields to the interest bill. How quickly higher rates pass through to federal interest costs as debt is refinanced.
  • Who holds the debt. Whether changes in Treasury ownership change how sensitive the premium is to fiscal conditions. The background is in Who Buys the Debt?
  • A fiscal-news diary. Market moves around one family of events, such as debt-limit deadlines or refunding announcements.
  • France as a separate case. The French-German ten-year spread measures something different from the US term premium, so it needs its own design rather than a copy of this one.

Data and code

The analysis uses public data from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the Federal Reserve Board, FRED and the Treasury. The code is kept in a private research repository; each piece publishes its generated results files, chart data and a record of the code version and data files used.

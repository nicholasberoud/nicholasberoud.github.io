Political Credibility and Sovereign Credit

What makes a government’s promise to repay credible?

Opened
24 October 2026
Updated
1 November 2026
Stage
Reading and choosing a feasible data question
Findings
No empirical results yet

A government may have the resources to repay its creditors. Whether it will choose to do so is a political question as well.

That distinction between ability and willingness is what drew me to sovereign credit. I want to understand how investors judge a government’s commitments, what institutional checks make those commitments more believable, and why governments with weak constraints can still find willing lenders. It connects my international political economy background with a concrete financing decision.

This is my developing degree-thesis direction. The final question, outcome, sample and contribution remain open. My current task is to examine what the available data can support before committing to a design.

What the anchor paper changed

Ballard-Rosa, Mosley and Wellhausen’s Contingent Advantage? puts global financing conditions alongside domestic institutions. Its outcomes are whether a government issues qualifying new debt and how much it issues. That is different from watching the price of an existing bond.

I have read it through once and discussed the argument; I skimmed the methods and am returning to them. I have not executed its replication. It is an intellectual starting point, so simply repeating its question with another institutional score would not establish a new contribution.

The question I am carrying forward is broader: when do political constraints make repayment more credible, and when might those same constraints make responding to financial trouble more difficult? This is a question to investigate in the literature, not an original finding.

A candidate research design

I am considering how institutional checks might affect the process of renegotiating sovereign debt. This is one possible route, not a chosen question.

Explore the provisional designQuestion, variables, pilot cases and rival explanations
Proposed direction · Not a settled thesis

This is one route to assess against the data and the closest literature. No models have been estimated and no institutional comparison has been established.

Working question. When an emerging-market government renegotiates external bond debt, are stronger pre-existing legislative checks associated with a shorter or longer restructuring process?

Mechanism to investigate. Creditors have to judge whether the government can deliver on a new repayment promise. Checks on an executive might help a negotiated commitment endure. They might also make it harder to agree on the fiscal changes or concessions needed to reach that commitment. The political question is which decisions are constrained, by whom, and at what stage.

Hypotheses. A commitment mechanism could help agreement by reassuring creditors that a deal will hold. An adjustment mechanism could delay agreement by making politically costly decisions harder to approve. The same institutional score cannot tell these stories apart. A dated account of proposals, approvals and negotiations would be needed alongside the quantitative comparison.

Plan. Start with that two-country coding exercise: identify the start and end rules, distinguish formal approvals from negotiation milestones, and document disagreements between sources. Do not infer market exclusion or unwillingness from a long spell. Only after inspecting coverage would I decide whether an episode-level descriptive comparison, a duration model or a different question is feasible.

Closest studies and possible contribution. Contingent Advantage? studies issuance and amounts under different global financing conditions. Hansen’s democratic (dis)advantage study is the next reading on the commitment–adjustment tension. An even closer overlap is Trebesch’s Resolving Sovereign Debt Crises: The Role of Political Risk, which already examines political risk and negotiation duration. I have identified it for reading, not yet worked through it. An open working-paper version is available. Asonuma and Trebesch’s restructuring data provide a starting point for defining the outcome. Studying the sequence of constrained decisions during negotiations might add something beyond an institutional score, but changing the dependent variable alone does not establish novelty. That literature check is unfinished.

Rival explanations. Initial debt distress, whether negotiations begin before or after default, creditor concentration, contract clauses, domestic financing, official support and global conditions could drive duration. The Argentina–Ecuador pilot also needs to account for different currency regimes and commodity exposure. Restricting the sample to restructurings selects countries already in trouble; it cannot establish whether institutional checks helped other governments avoid a crisis.

What would weaken the story? If negotiation delays track creditor coordination or financing needs while the allegedly constraining institutions did not block any relevant decision, the adjustment mechanism would have little support. An association between an index and duration would not rescue that missing mechanism.

Open question. Is restructuring duration a useful window onto political credibility, or does it mostly capture bargaining and crisis severity? Issuance remains an alternative outcome closer to the anchor paper. Neither outcome directly observes investors’ beliefs or governments’ repayment intentions.

Start with the data

The starting sources are Asonuma and Trebesch’s restructuring data in Christoph Trebesch’s catalogue and the V-Dem country-year dataset. Public downloads are available, but the relevant episode rows, instrument filter, institutional variation and merged sample have not yet been checked. Access is not the same as a usable design.

The IMF’s October 2020 regional outlook, Box 1, supplies context for the proposed Argentina and Ecuador pilot. It would be supplemented by public government and exchange documents for exact milestones. I would record each source’s date and definition rather than silently combine incompatible event dates.

Chinese official lending remains a separate possible direction, not part of this candidate sample. Ratings, spreads, issuance, debt-service burdens and restructuring outcomes answer different questions and will not be treated as interchangeable.

What I will do next

  1. Inspect the restructuring download and its documentation. Check the Argentina and Ecuador episode rows, event definitions, instrument categories and reuse conditions before building the pilot.
  2. Inspect the proposed political measure. Check the prior-year legislative-constraints scores and whether they capture the decisions in the pilot timelines.
  3. Check the closest literature. Read Hansen, review existing explanations of restructuring duration, and resolve the overlap with Yoo’s backsliding-and-ratings study before claiming an extension.
  4. Choose a bounded first exercise. Begin with a documented description or a clearly specified replication if feasible, then assess what a regression could and could not answer.

A replication with an extension remains a possible route. It is not yet a settled design or an accepted degree requirement.

My reading notes record what I have read, what caught my attention and what remains unclear. Hansen is next; Yoo’s full text remains an access problem. Those statuses matter as much as the bibliography.

Same Shock, Different Damage

Why does global stress hit some sovereign borrowers so much harder than others?

Started
24 October 2026
Stage
Question and design. No data collected, no results.
Scope
Emerging-market governments borrowing in dollars
WIP

This page shows the question and the plan as they stand. Nothing on it is a finding. It will change as the design is tested, and the changes will be dated.

When global money tightens, every emerging-market government feels it. Some see their borrowing costs barely move. Others lose access to markets, defend their currency in the middle of a recession, or end up asking the IMF for help. I want to know how much of that difference comes from political institutions, and how much from what a government owes and in which currency.

01The question

Do protections against political interference help explain why some governments see smaller increases in their dollar borrowing spreads during global financing stress?

The spread is the extra yield a government pays over a US Treasury of similar maturity. It is the market’s price for lending to that government rather than to the United States. A stress episode is a stretch of time when that price rises almost everywhere at once. The interesting part is that it doesn’t rise everywhere by the same amount.

02The puzzle

A recent New York Fed post by Hunter Clark, Jeffrey Dawson and Julian Gonzalez-Murphy sorts emerging markets into a core group and a periphery and finds that the core has held up much better through recent shocks. Their explanation points to macroeconomic and institutional reform, less borrowing in foreign currency, and larger reserves.

That is where my question starts, because those ingredients usually arrive together. A country that reformed its central bank often also learned to borrow in its own currency and built up reserves. Comparing two groups tells you the bundle works. It can’t tell you which ingredient did the work.

03How a weak currency becomes a credit problem

Start with a government that collects its taxes in pesos and owes a payment of one billion dollars next year. At 5 pesos to the dollar, the payment costs 5 billion pesos. If the peso weakens to 6, the same payment costs 6 billion. The debt hasn’t changed. The burden, measured against the government’s own income, has risen by a fifth.

The investor is still promised dollars. What changes is the investor’s confidence that the government can find them. If that confidence slips, the investor demands more to hold the bond, and its price falls before anything has gone wrong. That is how a currency move turns into a credit move.

Two ways a dollar bond’s yield rises

A dollar sovereign yield is a US Treasury yield plus a country spread. Suppose the Treasury yields 4% and the spread is 3 points, so the sovereign pays 7%.

If the Treasury rises to 5% and the spread holds, the sovereign pays 8%. Nothing about the country changed. If the spread also widens to 4, the sovereign pays 9%, and only that last point is the market repricing the country.

The numbers are hypothetical. The point is that a rise in a sovereign’s yield is not, on its own, evidence of rising country risk.

A government that owes a fixed amount in its own currency avoids that direct effect. It doesn’t escape everything: inflation, refinancing and foreign investors who sell local bonds after a currency loss can all still bite. But the most mechanical channel is gone.

04Where institutions come in

Now hold the debt fixed and change what people expect from the central bank. If investors believe it will stop a temporary inflation shock from becoming permanent, and won’t print money to cover the deficit, a weaker currency is less likely to start a spiral of inflation, more depreciation and financial stress. The repayment outlook holds up better, and so, possibly, does the spread.

The chain I want to test runs like this:

legal protection → more believable commitments → smaller inflation and currency spiral → better repayment outlook → smaller spread increase

Each arrow is a claim, not a fact. Two cautions shape the design. First, independence written into law is not the same as independence in practice. Second, it is easy to go in a circle: call a country credible because its spreads are low, then use credibility to explain the low spreads. The institution has to be measured before, and separately from, the market outcome.

05The plan

A small, honest version first. If the data hold up, a larger one later.

  1. One episode.Start with 2022, when global financing tightened sharply. It is a period of global stress, not a clean experiment: many things happened at once.
  2. A handful of countries.A small Latin American comparison, chosen before looking at how their spreads moved.
  3. One outcome.The change in each country’s dollar sovereign spread over the episode, in basis points.
  4. One institution, measured beforehand.A legal measure of central-bank independence from before the episode, such as Carolina Garriga’s dataset.
  5. The rivals, side by side.The foreign-currency share of government debt, reserves and commodity exposure, each measured before the episode.
  6. Check the data before trusting it.Public country spread series exist, for example from Peru’s central bank. Each needs its units, coverage and terms of use confirmed before anything is charted.

06What this will not be able to show

  • Cause. With a few countries and one episode, the result is a description. Institutions, debt structure and reserves move together, and a handful of cases can’t pull them apart.
  • The whole financing cost. Dollar spreads are one price. Local-currency yields answer a related but different question.
  • A clean channel. Debt structure may be part of how institutions work, so controlling for it could hide the very effect I am looking for.

07What already exists

None of this starts from zero. These are the pieces I need to understand before claiming anything is new.

  • Clark, Dawson and Gonzalez-Murphy, A Closer Look at Emerging Market Resilience During Recent Shocks, Liberty Street Economics, 9 April 2026.
  • IMF, World Economic Outlook, October 2025, chapter 2, on whether emerging-market resilience reflects good policy or good luck.
  • IMF Working Paper, The Credibility Premium: Central Bank Independence and Local-Currency Sovereign Yields, March 2026. The closest existing study; it looks at local-currency yields, not dollar spreads.
  • Eichengreen and Hausmann, Exchange Rates and Financial Fragility, NBER, 1999, where “original sin”, the inability to borrow abroad in your own currency, enters the debate.
  • BIS, Original sin redux: a model-based evaluation, on why local-currency debt still carries risk when foreigners hold it.
  • Garriga, Central Bank Independence dataset. Legal independence only, and the current version ends in 2023.

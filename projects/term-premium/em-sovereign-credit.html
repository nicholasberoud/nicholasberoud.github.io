02Candidate Questions and Mechanisms

Several questions are still in play. They overlap, and at most one or two will survive contact with the data and the literature.

  1. HypothesisInstitutions and resilience under stress.When global financing tightens, governments with stronger protections against political interference in monetary policy see smaller increases in their borrowing costs.
  2. HypothesisPolicy change and credibility.Markets reprice a sovereign when its institutions change, for example a central-bank reform, a new fiscal rule or a change of government, and how much depends on whether the change is believed.
  3. HypothesisHow governments borrow.Credible institutions let governments borrow more in their own currency and at longer maturities, which in turn leaves them more room to respond to shocks.
  4. Open questionDomestic credibility and outside support.Does international backing, such as a precautionary IMF arrangement, add to a government’s own credibility or stand in for it?

The common thread is a chain from institutions to beliefs to prices. Hypothesis Rules that limit political interference make policy commitments more believable; believable commitments lower the risk investors price in; and that shows up in borrowing costs and in the terms on which a government can borrow. Each link is a claim to test, not an assumption.

03What Could Be Measured

Plan Candidate variables, none of them chosen yet.

Possible variables. Each would be measured before the outcome it is meant to explain.
RoleCandidates
Outcome (dependent)Change in dollar sovereign spreads during a stress episode; local-currency government bond yields; rating changes; access to new issuance
Institution (independent)Legal central-bank independence; fiscal rules; dated institutional reforms or changes of government
Rival factorsForeign-currency share of government debt; reserves; commodity exposure; growth and inflation history
Global conditionsUS rates and the dollar; global risk appetite

One distinction matters throughout: independence written into law is not the same as independence in practice, and credibility is a belief, not a rule. The design has to measure the institution separately from, and before, the market outcome. Otherwise it is easy to call a country credible because its spreads are low, then explain the low spreads with credibility.

Competing explanations Any result would have to survive these:

  • Global conditions. US rates, the dollar and risk appetite move all emerging-market spreads together.
  • Commodities. The same shock helps exporters and hurts importers.
  • Buffers and debt structure. Reserves and foreign-currency debt may explain resilience on their own, or may be the route through which institutions work.
  • Reverse causality. Good times make reform easier, so reforming countries may already have been doing well.
  • Circularity. Ratings and some governance measures already include judgements about institutions.

05Selected Readings and Open Questions

Starting points, chosen for the question rather than as a finished review.

  • Clark, Dawson and Gonzalez-Murphy, A Closer Look at Emerging Market Resilience During Recent Shocks, Liberty Street Economics, 9 April 2026. Splits emerging markets into a core and a periphery and points to institutional reform, less foreign-currency borrowing and larger reserves.
  • IMF, World Economic Outlook, October 2025, chapter 2, on whether emerging-market resilience reflects good policy or good luck.
  • IMF Working Paper, The Credibility Premium: Central Bank Independence and Local-Currency Sovereign Yields, March 2026. The closest existing study.
  • Eichengreen and Hausmann, Exchange Rates and Financial Fragility, NBER, 1999, on “original sin”: the inability to borrow abroad in your own currency.

Open questions

  • Which institution, and which outcome? Spreads, yields, ratings and market access answer different questions.
  • Which countries and which period, chosen before looking at the results?
  • Can a small comparison say anything beyond description, and if not, is a documented case study more honest?
  • What would be new, given that the closest IMF work already studies central-bank independence and local-currency yields?
